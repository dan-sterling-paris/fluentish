Pronunciation Tips
Singing off the back teeth

Imagine your back teeth and widen everything out. It feels forced at first, but overdo it and then bring it back. This is the technique that takes you from "good" to fluent.

French Rs

Don't roll it too much, and don't do a hard English R. Just chew on it lightly. A light English R is better than a bad French one.

Point vs Pont

Sur le point de (on the point of) vs sur le pont (on the bridge). The nasal sounds matter! Think "pwan" not "pon". Like the duck noise: quang quang.

Le français vs La française

Le français = the French language (masculine). Une française = a French woman (feminine). Be careful which one you mean!

Silent final letters

Les muscardins - don't pronounce the final S. Same for most French plurals. The "les" already tells you it's plural.

On vs Nous

In casual conversation, use on instead of nous. Nous sounds too formal for everyday French. On a vécu (we lived) not nous avons vécu.

Pendant, not Pour

For duration, use pendant (during/for), not pour. Saying "pour un an" is a classic English-speaker giveaway.

La France (feminine!)

La France, never "le France". The French won't care if you get Canada or Portugal wrong, but they will notice this one. Your protective armour.

Character & delivery

Get into character when you speak. Relax your shoulders, throw the phrase out like it's nothing. Your delivery transforms when you embody the intent rather than recite the words.

Ménage vs Déménager

Ménage = cleaning. Déménager = to move house. Similar etymology, completely different meanings.

Comédien, not acteur

Acteur/actrice can be slang for a pornographic actor. The safe word for theatre and film is comédien/comédienne. Comedians (as we mean them) are humoristes.

Est-ce que = beer mat learning

Stick est-ce que in front of any statement to turn it into a question. No complex grammar needed. Je peux aller (I can go) becomes est-ce que je peux aller ? (can I go?). Works with everything.

Market politeness

Markets are polite, Sunday environments. Always bonjour when arriving, merci, bonne journée when leaving. Use je peux prendre (can I take) to order politely. The French notice when you skip the pleasantries.

Infer, don't translate

Don't try to understand every word. Pick up the key words and infer the rest from context, hand gestures, and tone. Even native speakers miss words in fast speech. Your ear is better than you think.

Output Hypothesis

Speaking beats passive listening for building fluency. Forcing yourself to produce language (even badly) creates stronger neural pathways than absorbing input. The "noticing gap" happens when you try to say something, get stuck, and then hear the correction. That gap is where real learning lives.

Shadowing technique

Pick a 30-second clip (cartoons, Friends in French, YouTube). Play it, pause, repeat it exactly as you heard it. Don't worry about understanding every word. Focus on matching the rhythm, pronunciation, and intonation. Do this daily.

The frustration is the learning

That lag you feel when searching for words is your brain doing the translation work in real time. It's not a sign of failure. The embarrassment of getting stuck triggers a stress response, but pushing through is exactly what builds procedural memory.

"On" is your best friend

On replaces nous in everyday French. Master three structures: on est en train de (present), on a (past), on va (future). On va is especially useful because you just add the infinitive, no conjugation needed.

Weaving (code-switching)

Native French speakers mix in English words when the English term works better. "On va regarder le match live" is completely natural. If you're stuck, weave in the English word and keep going. Flow beats perfection.

Ditch the notebook (during conversation)

Writing is great for grammar study, but during conversation it becomes a crutch. Your ear needs the workout. Focus on listening and speaking. The portal has your notes for review afterwards.

When asked a question, just keep talking

Don't panic when someone asks you a question in French. Even if you didn't fully catch it, just answer with whatever you can. In polite conversation, they won't correct you. Silence is what makes people switch to English.

Voyage vs Trajet

Un voyage is a long journey (Paris to Marseille). Un trajet is a short one (a commute, a train ride). Trajet comes from "trajectory".

Carte grise vs UK logbook

The carte grise is France's equivalent of the V5C. French car insurance typically covers the person rather than the car (unlike the UK).

Frame and slot thinking

Stop translating whole sentences word by word. Automate the frame (il y a des, je pense que c'est, on ne sait pas) and focus all your brainpower on the slot (the noun, adjective, or verb). The frame should fire automatically; the slot is where you do the real work.

Priming your ear

Saying a frame dozens of times doesn't just improve your output. It primes your brain to hear it when someone says it back. Once you recognise "il y a des" instantly, you skip straight to the important noun that follows.

Weaving = complex sentences

Link two or three frames with connectors (mais, parce que, et) to produce sophisticated-sounding French. It feels clunky at first, but the mechanism is what matters. Fluency comes from repetition.

Lupin for ear training

Watch Lupin (Omar Sy) with French audio and French subtitles. Subtitles sometimes differ from the spoken dialogue, which is normal. Focus on spotting the frames you've drilled: il y a des, je pense que c'est, on ne sait pas.

English has more words, French has more structure

English has a far larger vocabulary (think of all the words for "wet": damp, soggy, soaked, moist, drenched). French compensates with more structured expressions. Don't look for 1:1 translations; look for the French way to express the idea.

Galérer = the feeling of struggling

On galère is colloquial French for "we're struggling / it's a slog". Perfect for renovation, language learning, or bureaucracy. Very natural and widely used.

Pronunciation Notes from This Lesson
"Le point" nasal sound

Think of how French ducks go "quang quang" - it's that nasal. If you say "pont" instead of "point", you're saying bridge instead of point. Overdo the nasality, then bring it back.

"Muscardin" endings

Words ending in -in have a nasal sound: muscardin, like vin (wine). Really lean into it. And don't pronounce the final S in the plural.

"Doucement" not "douche"!

Doucement = gently/slowly. Douche = a shower. Small but important difference! The "-ment" ending is soft: "dooss-mon".

Grammar & Pronunciation Notes
On vs Nous

When talking about your family or in casual conversation, use on instead of nous. Nous is technically correct but sounds too formal for everyday French, a bit like saying "one does" in English. On a vécu (we lived) not nous avons vécu.

Pendant, not Pour

For duration, use pendant (during/for), not pour. Saying "pour un an" is a classic English-speaker giveaway. On a vécu pendant un an à Paris or simply on a vécu un an à Paris.

La France (feminine!)

France is feminine: la France, never "le France". The French are fiercely proud of this. They won't care if you get Canada or Portugal wrong, but if you say "le France" they will notice. This is your protective armour.

Character & Delivery

Get into character when you speak. Like an actor changing emphasis on "To be or not to be", your delivery changes when you embody the intent. Shake your head, relax your shoulders, throw the phrase out like it's nothing. It transforms your pronunciation.

Ménage vs Déménager

Ménage = cleaning (faire le ménage). Déménager = to move house. The etymology looks similar but the meanings are completely different. Amener = to bring, so dé-ménager is literally to un-bring your home.

Tips & Notes from This Lesson
Beer mat learning (est-ce que)

Stick est-ce que in front of any statement to make it a question. Je peux aller (I can go) becomes est-ce que je peux aller ? (can I go?). Swap the verb to build dozens of questions: est-ce que je dois (do I have to), est-ce que je vais (am I going to), est-ce que je veux (do I want). Add comment (how) or pourquoi (why) for even more.

Comédien, not acteur

Acteur/actrice can be slang for a pornographic actor in French. The proper word for a theatre or film actor is comédien/comédienne. And what we call a "comedian" in English is an humoriste in French.

Market politeness

Markets are a little flashback to old-fashioned, polite France. Always bonjour first, merci, bonne journée when leaving. Use je peux prendre (can I take) to order politely. The French get spiky if you skip the pleasantries.

Incendie vs feu

Un incendie is a wildfire or serious fire. Un feu is a general fire but also means traffic lights (le feu rouge). For current events, les incendies is what they're saying on the news.

Infer, don't translate

You don't need to understand every word. The archduchess tongue twister proved it: you picked up "socks" from a stream of rapid French. In conversation, grab the key words and infer context from gestures and tone. Your comprehension is much higher than you think.

Taille vs longueur

La taille = size (general). La longueur = length. Both useful when ordering wood for your son's business. "Quatre fois la taille de Paris" = four times the size of Paris.

Tips & Notes from This Lesson
Output Hypothesis

Speaking beats passive listening for building fluency. Researched by Merrill Swain in Quebec in the 1960s-80s: forcing yourself to produce language (even badly) creates stronger neural pathways than just absorbing input. The "noticing gap" happens when you try to say something, get stuck, and then hear the correction. That gap is where real learning lives.

Shadowing technique

Pick a 30-second clip (cartoons, Friends in French, YouTube channels with fast then slow speech). Play it, pause, repeat it exactly as you heard it. Don't worry about understanding every word. Focus on matching the rhythm, pronunciation, and intonation. Do this daily.

The frustration is the learning

That lag you feel when searching for words is your brain doing the translation work in real time. It's not a sign of failure, it's a sign of neural processing. Dan compared it to fight-or-flight: the embarrassment of getting stuck triggers the same stress response, but pushing through is exactly what builds procedural memory.

"On" is your best friend

On replaces nous in everyday French. Master three structures and you can talk about anything in any tense: on est en train de (present, "we're doing"), on a (past, "we did"), on va (future, "we're going to"). Next lesson will apply these to avoir (to have).

Carte grise vs UK logbook

The carte grise is France's equivalent of the V5C. It proves ownership of the vehicle. French car insurance typically covers the person rather than the car (unlike the UK), but this is worth verifying with your insurer.

Tips & Notes from This Lesson
Frame and slot thinking

Stop trying to translate whole sentences word by word. Instead, automate the frame (the structural glue: il y a des, je pense que c'est, on ne sait pas) and focus all your brainpower on the slot (the noun, adjective, or verb that carries the actual meaning). The frame should fire automatically; the slot is where you do the real work.

Priming your ear

Saying a frame dozens of times doesn't just improve your output. It primes your brain to hear it when someone says it back to you. Once you recognise "il y a des" instantly, you can skip straight to the important noun that follows it.

Weaving = complex sentences

Link two or three frames with connectors (mais, parce que, et) and you suddenly sound like you're producing sophisticated French. It feels clunky at first, but the mechanism is what matters. Fluency comes from repetition.

N-gram analysis: why these frames?

Dan analysed hours of real spoken French (influencers, podcasts, interviews) and measured not just frequency but dispersion, how evenly a phrase appears across different conversations. "Il y a" appeared more than double the next most common trigram. These aren't textbook phrases; they're the building blocks of how French is actually spoken.

Lupin for ear training

Watch Lupin (with Omar Sy) with French audio and French subtitles. The subtitles sometimes differ from the spoken dialogue, which is normal. Focus on spotting the frames you've drilled: il y a des, je pense que c'est, on ne sait pas. Hearing them in context reinforces the automaticity.

English has more words, French has more structure

English has a far larger vocabulary than French (think of all the words for "wet": damp, soggy, soaked, moist, drenched). French compensates with more structured expressions. Don't look for a 1:1 translation; look for the French way to express the idea.

Galérer = the feeling of struggling

On galère is colloquial French for "we're struggling / it's a slog". Perfect for describing renovation, language learning, or navigating bureaucracy. Very natural and widely used.
