Pronunciation Tips
Singing off the back teeth

Imagine your back teeth and widen everything out. It feels forced at first, but overdo it and then bring it back. This is the technique that takes you from "good" to fluent.

French Rs

Don't roll it too much, and don't do a hard English R. Just chew on it lightly. A light English R is better than a bad French one.

Point vs Pont

Sur le point de (on the point of) vs sur le pont (on the bridge). The nasal sounds matter! Think "pwan" not "pon". Like the duck noise: quang quang.

Le français vs La française

Le français = the French language (masculine). Une française = a French woman (feminine). Be careful which one you mean!

Silent final letters

Les muscardins - don't pronounce the final S. Same for most French plurals. The "les" already tells you it's plural.

On vs Nous

In casual conversation, use on instead of nous. Nous sounds too formal for everyday French. On a vécu (we lived) not nous avons vécu.

Pendant, not Pour

For duration, use pendant (during/for), not pour. Saying "pour un an" is a classic English-speaker giveaway.

La France (feminine!)

La France, never "le France". The French won't care if you get Canada or Portugal wrong, but they will notice this one. Your protective armour.

Character & delivery

Get into character when you speak. Relax your shoulders, throw the phrase out like it's nothing. Your delivery transforms when you embody the intent rather than recite the words.

Ménage vs Déménager

Ménage = cleaning. Déménager = to move house. Similar etymology, completely different meanings.

Comédien, not acteur

Acteur/actrice can be slang for a pornographic actor. The safe word for theatre and film is comédien/comédienne. Comedians (as we mean them) are humoristes.

Est-ce que = beer mat learning

Stick est-ce que in front of any statement to turn it into a question. No complex grammar needed. Je peux aller (I can go) becomes est-ce que je peux aller ? (can I go?). Works with everything.

Market politeness

Markets are polite, Sunday environments. Always bonjour when arriving, merci, bonne journée when leaving. Use je peux prendre (can I take) to order politely. The French notice when you skip the pleasantries.

Infer, don't translate

Don't try to understand every word. Pick up the key words and infer the rest from context, hand gestures, and tone. Even native speakers miss words in fast speech. Your ear is better than you think.

Pronunciation Notes from This Lesson
"Le point" nasal sound

Think of how French ducks go "quang quang" - it's that nasal. If you say "pont" instead of "point", you're saying bridge instead of point. Overdo the nasality, then bring it back.

"Muscardin" endings

Words ending in -in have a nasal sound: muscardin, like vin (wine). Really lean into it. And don't pronounce the final S in the plural.

"Doucement" not "douche"!

Doucement = gently/slowly. Douche = a shower. Small but important difference! The "-ment" ending is soft: "dooss-mon".

Grammar & Pronunciation Notes
On vs Nous

When talking about your family or in casual conversation, use on instead of nous. Nous is technically correct but sounds too formal for everyday French, a bit like saying "one does" in English. On a vécu (we lived) not nous avons vécu.

Pendant, not Pour

For duration, use pendant (during/for), not pour. Saying "pour un an" is a classic English-speaker giveaway. On a vécu pendant un an à Paris or simply on a vécu un an à Paris.

La France (feminine!)

France is feminine: la France, never "le France". The French are fiercely proud of this. They won't care if you get Canada or Portugal wrong, but if you say "le France" they will notice. This is your protective armour.

Character & Delivery

Get into character when you speak. Like an actor changing emphasis on "To be or not to be", your delivery changes when you embody the intent. Shake your head, relax your shoulders, throw the phrase out like it's nothing. It transforms your pronunciation.

Ménage vs Déménager

Ménage = cleaning (faire le ménage). Déménager = to move house. The etymology looks similar but the meanings are completely different. Amener = to bring, so dé-ménager is literally to un-bring your home.

Tips & Notes from This Lesson
Beer mat learning (est-ce que)

Stick est-ce que in front of any statement to make it a question. Je peux aller (I can go) becomes est-ce que je peux aller ? (can I go?). Swap the verb to build dozens of questions: est-ce que je dois (do I have to), est-ce que je vais (am I going to), est-ce que je veux (do I want). Add comment (how) or pourquoi (why) for even more.

Comédien, not acteur

Acteur/actrice can be slang for a pornographic actor in French. The proper word for a theatre or film actor is comédien/comédienne. And what we call a "comedian" in English is an humoriste in French.

Market politeness

Markets are a little flashback to old-fashioned, polite France. Always bonjour first, merci, bonne journée when leaving. Use je peux prendre (can I take) to order politely. The French get spiky if you skip the pleasantries.

Incendie vs feu

Un incendie is a wildfire or serious fire. Un feu is a general fire but also means traffic lights (le feu rouge). For current events, les incendies is what they're saying on the news.

Infer, don't translate

You don't need to understand every word. The archduchess tongue twister proved it: you picked up "socks" from a stream of rapid French. In conversation, grab the key words and infer context from gestures and tone. Your comprehension is much higher than you think.

Taille vs longueur

La taille = size (general). La longueur = length. Both useful when ordering wood for your son's business. "Quatre fois la taille de Paris" = four times the size of Paris.

La Fête de la Musique fet duh lah mü-zeek Music festival – 21 juin (the solstice)

At the Market

le marché mar-shay the market
le marchand mar-shon the merchant / stallholder
l'endroit lon-drwah the place / the spot
les vacanciers vah-kon-syay tourists / holidaymakers (non-judgmental)

At the Restaurant

une carafe kah-raf a jug of tap water (free)
une bouteille boo-tay a bottle (implies you'll pay for it)
un panneau pah-noh a sign (e.g. on a wall or post)
une affiche ah-feesh a poster
Dialogue — Asking for Water
Maria
Excusez-moi — on peut avoir une carafe d'eau, s'il vous plaît ?
Excuse me — could we have a jug of water, please?
Serveur
Bien sûr, madame. Je vous apporte ça tout de suite.
Of course, madam. I'll bring that right away.
Maria
Merci beaucoup !
Thank you very much!
