Vocabulary

Farm & Horses

Un cheval shuh-val A horse
Des chevaux day shuh-vo Horses (plural, irregular!)
Une ferme oon fairm A farm
Un fils un fees A son (the final S is pronounced, unusually for French)

Writing & Literature

Une écrivaine oon ay-kree-ven A writer (feminine, the one you want)
Un roman un ro-man A novel
Un livre un leevr A book
Un nom de plume un nom duh ploom A pen name (plume = feather, as in a quill)
La littérature féminine la lee-tay-ra-toor fay-mee-neen Women's fiction / chick lit

Verbs: Écrire (to write)

J'écris zhay-kree I write (present). Watch the nasal vowel: not "glace"!
J'ai écrit zhay ay-kree I wrote / I have written (past). Sounds nearly the same spoken.

The spelling changes (S → T at the end) but the pronunciation barely does. When it's spoken, you don't have to worry about which one you're using.

Sounding Natural

Accent fundamentals

Your Welsh vowels are already doing you favours: that rounded mouth shape is closer to French than flat English is. The main habit to build: relax the R and keep sounds further back in the mouth.

Merci beaucoup
mair-see bo-koo
Thank you very much

Watch the "beaucoup": rush the vowels and it drifts toward two words you really don't want to say to a waiter. Keep it deep and round: bo​-koo, not bo-kew.

The "On" Trick

In textbooks, "we" is nous. In real life, almost nobody says nous in casual conversation. The French say on instead. On va à Paris (we're going to Paris), on mange (we're eating). It also works as a vague "people" or "one": on me considère (people consider me). One word, two jobs, and it makes you sound immediately less like a textbook.

A Little Etymology
Un compagnon
un kom-pan-yon
A companion

Com- (with) + pain (bread): a companion is literally someone you share bread with. A lot of French vocabulary is hiding in plain sight like this once you know where to look for the English cousin underneath the accent.

Also: if you ever see a bottle of "La Vieille Ferme" (the rooster label) in the supermarket, now you know exactly what it means. The Old Farm.

Technique: The Window Pane

Think of stretching pizza dough. You work it a little, it goes slightly transparent. Keep going, a bit more transparent. Keep going until a hole appears. That's where you need more practice.

1

Take one phrase at a time. Say it five times as fast as you can. Don't worry about accuracy, just get through it.

2

Stretch to two phrases. Repeat until smooth. When a hole appears (you trip), stop there and drill that join.

3

Keep stretching until you can run the whole script in one go. Speed first, polish later.

You're a visual learner: you see the shape of the sentence. So record yourself reading the script correctly, then listen and repeat. That links the shape to the sound, and the sound is what will come out when you need it.
