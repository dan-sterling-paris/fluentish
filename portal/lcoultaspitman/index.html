Frames & Fillers

15 Sep 2026 · 3 frames · 8 modifiers · 21 filler words
What You Covered

Three foundational frames: il y a des ___ (there are some), je pense que c'est ___ (I think it's), and il y a [modifier] de ___ (there are lots/too many/never any of). You proved that the cognitive load sits on the English, not the French, by rattling off 20 nouns in a minute without hesitating on the frame.

You then learned 8 modifiers that slot into the third frame, giving you 168 sentence combinations.

You also explored the past tense shortcut (add an "é" sound), singular forms of "il y a", and the bonus frame "je me suis dit" which sounds far more natural than the textbook "j'ai pensé".

Key Concepts

The Past Tense Shortcut

Add an "é" sound to make most verbs past tense. Don't overthink conjugation.

Je pense → J'ai pensé zhuh ponss → zhay pon-say I think → I thought
Je mange → J'ai mangé zhuh monzh → zhay mon-zhay I eat → I ate

Making "Il y a" Singular

Replace "des" with "un" (masculine), "une" (feminine), or "du/de la" (uncountable).

Il y a des fleurs → Il y a une fleur
ee-lee-ah day flur → ee-lee-ah oon flur
There are flowers → There is a flower
Il y a de la neige
ee-lee-ah duh la nezh
There is some snow

The Canada Story

You say "en France" (feminine country) but "au Canada" (masculine country). There's no intuitive rule. Keep this locked and loaded for when anyone criticises your French.

Lesson 2

The Beer Mat

5 Oct 2026 · 3 core verbs · future shortcut · gender & destinations
What You Covered

Three foundational verbs — être, avoir, aller — are the engine behind most of French. You can write them on a beer mat and they'll get you surprisingly far.

You also learned the future shortcut: je vais + any infinitive. No complex conjugation needed — just "I'm going to [verb]" and slot in whatever you want to do.

Plus: the gender rule for destinations (au for masculine, à la for feminine), and the circumlocution toolkit for when the right word won't come.

The Future Shortcut

Je vais + infinitive

No conjugation tables. Just "I'm going to" + any verb in its plain form.

Infinitives to know

allerto go
mangerto eat
faireto do / make
étudierto study
parlerto speak
rentrerto go home
regarderto watch
travaillerto work

So you get sentences like

Je vais manger
zhuh vay mon-zhay
I'm going to eat
Je vais faire du shopping
zhuh vay fair doo sho-ping
I'm going to do some shopping
Je vais rentrer
zhuh vay ron-tray
I'm going to head home

Add re- to the front of most verbs to mean "again" — refaire = to redo, retourner = to go back.

Getting Around: Au vs À la

Gender decides the preposition

Masculine places take au. Feminine places take à la. Countries follow the same rule.

au + masculine oh au cinéma, au Canada, au marché
à la + feminine ah lah à la piscine, en France, à la maison

Feminine (à la / en)

The Frequency Monologue

29 Oct 2026 · 7 lines · 10% → 15% → 20% frequency thresholds
What Is Frequency Training?

The most common 10% of French words account for roughly half of everything a native speaker says. The top 20% covers the vast majority of everyday conversation. This script was generated using those exact frequency rankings.

By memorising this monologue you're not just learning 7 sentences — you're internalising the most statistically useful sounds, rhythms, and liaisons in the language.

Each section builds on the previous one. Start at 10%, get it solid, then extend to 15%, then 20%.

How to Practise
1

Read it aloud. Don't mumble it in your head — say it properly, every time.

2

Look away. Close this page and try to say the line from memory.

3

Check and repeat. Open it back up, correct anything, and say it again.

4

Sleep on it. Don't cram. A short session today and tomorrow beats two hours in one go.

10% threshold

Your Opening Lines

Start here. These two lines alone will open almost any conversation in France.

Bonjour ! Je suis Louise, je suis anglaise.
bon-zhoor · zhuh swee loo-eez · zhuh swee on-glez
Hello! I'm Louise, I'm English.
Le français est pas parfait, mais pas de panique !
luh fron-say ay pah par-fay · may pah duh pah-neek
My French isn't perfect, but don't panic!

Pronunciation note

Je suis often becomes "chui" at native speed (shwee). Either version is fine. est pas (ay pah) drops the "ne" — this is normal spoken French, not sloppy.

15% threshold

The Warmup Questions

Once the opener is solid, add these two lines.

Vous êtes de la région ?
voo-zet duh lah ray-zhyon
Are you from around here?
Je pense que vous avez de la chance : je parle lentement.
zhuh ponss kuh voo-za-vay duh lah shonss · zhuh parl lont-mon
I think you're lucky: I speak slowly.

Pronunciation note

Vous êtes — the s links to the ê, giving "voo-zet". vous avez — same liaison, "voo-za-vay". These links are what make French sound French.

20% threshold

The Full Monologue

Add these three lines to complete the script.

Vous pouvez me dire tu, c'est plus simple.
voo poo-vay muh deer too · say ploo sam-pluh
You can use "tu" with me, it's simpler.
Tu es à côté de Londres ? Moi, je suis à Ealing, à côté de Londres.
too ay ah koh-tay duh lond-ruh · mwah · zhuh swee ah ee-ling · ah koh-tay duh lond-ruh
Are you near London? I'm in Ealing, near London.
C'est un coin calme, c'est pas Paris.
say-tan kwan kalm · say pah pa-ree
It's a quiet little spot, it's no Paris.

Pronunciation note

C'est un links into "say-tan". c'est pas — the t is silent, so "say pah". à côté de = "ah koh-tay duh" — the accent on the é keeps it clean and open.
