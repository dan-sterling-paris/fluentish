Frames, fillers & past tense

Frames & Fillers: The Idea

Most people try to build French sentences word by word, translating from English in their head. It's slow, exhausting, and it sounds unnatural. The frames and fillers method flips this around.

A frame is a fixed chunk of sentence that crops up constantly in real spoken French. Things like il y a des (there are some), je pense que c'est (I think it's), je me suis dit (I said to myself). These come from n-gram analysis of transcribed French speech corpora, not from textbooks.

A filler (or "slot") is the variable part: the noun, adjective, or clause you drop into the frame. That's where the meaning lives.

The trick: drill the frame until it fires automatically, like muscle memory. Then your brain only has to find one word (the filler) instead of assembling an entire sentence from scratch. Three frames and a handful of nouns gives you dozens of natural-sounding sentences, instantly.

Tips & Notes
Frame and slot thinking

Stop trying to translate whole sentences word by word. Automate the frame (the structural glue: il y a des, je pense que c'est, je me suis dit) and focus all your brainpower on the slot (the noun, adjective, or clause that carries the actual meaning). The frame should fire automatically; the slot is where you do the real work.

Priming your ear

Saying a frame dozens of times doesn't just improve your output. It primes your brain to hear it when someone says it back to you. Once you recognise "il y a des" instantly, you can skip straight to the important noun that follows it.

Weaving = complex sentences

Link two or three frames with connectors (mais, parce que, et) and you suddenly sound like you're producing sophisticated French. It feels clunky at first, but the mechanism is what matters. Fluency comes from repetition.

Why these frames?

Dan ran n-gram extraction across transcribed French speech corpora and measured not just frequency but dispersion, how evenly a phrase appears across different speakers and conversations. "Il y a" appeared more than double the next most common trigram. These aren't textbook phrases; they're the building blocks of how French is actually spoken.

The seesaw: aller and rentrer

Je suis allée (I went) and je suis rentrée (I came back) are a mirror image: out and back. Point one finger out and bring it back as you say them. It feels ridiculous, but the physical cue is what makes the pair fire together later, and eventually you drop the gesture and the words come on their own.

The liaison z (and where not to put it)

Je suis allée gets a z sound joining the two words: zhuh swee za-lay. That's because allée starts with a vowel. Je suis rentrée gets no z, because rentrée starts with a consonant: zhuh swee ron-tray. Sprinkling extra z's where they don't belong is one of the small things that gives you away.

Learn it as one word

Don't unpick je suis allée into "I am gone" — that route through English is what slows you down. Je suis allée = "I went". One word, one meaning. Think of a small child saying "more juice": they have no idea that more is a quantity and juice is squeezed oranges. They just know the chunk gets them the thing. That's the level of automation you're after.

"I am ...ing" is a false friend

French has no separate "I am eating" form. You just use the present tense: je mange (I eat / I'm eating), je regarde (I watch / I'm watching). Trying to build the "am" part is what tips you into je suis en train de... territory, and you don't need it.

Mon amie vs mes amis

Mon amie (mon-a-mee) is one friend. Mes amis (may za-mee) is several. And keep the avec in: je mange avec mes amis. Drop it and you've announced that you're eating your friends.

Beaucoup, carefully

It's boh-koo, with a full round "oo" at the end. Clip it to boh-kuh and you've said beau cul — "nice bum". So merci beaucoup said carelessly becomes "thanks, nice bum". Worth a laugh with Catherine and Cynthia, and worth getting right with everyone else.

Rhythm beats accuracy

When a sentence is correct but still sounds clunky, the problem is pacing, not vocabulary. Parrot the sounds back without attaching any meaning to them — treat it as music rather than language. Flow is what makes people hear you as a French speaker.

Your Past-Tense Pair

A small group of French verbs — the movement ones — build the past with je suis rather than j'ai. These two are the pair to own first.

Je suis allée... (+ verb or place)
zhuh swee za-lay
I went...
Je suis rentrée... (+ place)
zhuh swee ron-tray
I came back / I got home...

Note on the spelling: you'll see allé and rentré written with an extra -e for a woman — allée, rentrée. It's completely silent. Nothing changes in your mouth.

Pronunciation Fixes from This Lesson
The liaison z — and where it doesn't go

Je suis allée joins up with a z: zhuh swee za-lay, because allée starts with a vowel. Je suis rentrée gets no z, because rentrée starts with a consonant. You were adding z's into places they don't belong, and that's exactly the kind of small thing that makes a French ear pause.

Nager

Two syllables, nothing more: na-zhay. Something extra was creeping in and polluting it. Strip it back — na-zhay, clean, no consonants smuggled into the middle.

Rentrée

ron-tray. It helps to know where it comes from: entrer is to enter, so rentrer is literally to re-enter — the same re- you already know from au revoir (until I see you again). You're saying "I went, and I re-entered".

Chez, not "à chez"

Chez does the whole job on its own. Chez mon amie, chez Catherine, chez moi — nothing goes in front of it, and the person's name goes straight after.

Beaucoup, carefully

boh-koo, with a full round "oo". Clipped to boh-kuh it becomes beau cul — "nice bum". Merci beaucoup is worth getting right.

Grammar & Notes
Don't translate it — learn it as one word

Je suis allée is not "I am gone". The moment you try to decode it through English you've lost the speed. Je suis allée = "I went". Je suis rentrée = "I came back". One chunk, one meaning, no working out.

"I am eating" doesn't exist in French

English has a separate form for "I am ...ing". French doesn't — it just uses the present tense. Je mange covers both "I eat" and "I'm eating"; je regarde covers "I watch" and "I'm watching". The encouraging part: you already had manger and regarder. It was only the "am" that was tripping you.

Mon amie / mes amis — and keep the avec

Mon amie (mon-a-mee) is one friend; mes amis (may za-mee) is several. And say je mange avec mes amis. Without the avec, you've just said you're eating them.

Petit déjeuner

Breakfast is le petit déjeuner — literally "the little lunch". Which makes lunch itself simply le déjeuner.

Three strikes, then two lives

Dan stopped correcting on the spot and gave you three attempts before stepping in, then narrowed it to two on nager and rentrer. Knowing the lives were limited slowed you down — and you got them first time. That mild pressure is deliberate: it's a rehearsal for the real conversation, where people's patience is finite too.

Flipped frame and filler

Keep the French word constant and let everything else be English: "I can nager, I want to nager, I have nagé, the dog can nager, the children will nager." All your attention goes on the English, so the French word has to come out on its own. Then you do the same with rentrer.

The seesaw gesture

One finger out for je suis allée, back in for je suis rentrée. A physical cue feels absurd, and it works: after enough repetitions the gesture drops away and the pair arrives together on its own. Do it in the shower, cooking, with a glass of wine in hand.

Parroting for flow

Repeat the sentence after Dan without attaching any meaning to it — just copy the sounds, like music. Meaning is what makes you slow down and translate. Take it away and the rhythm turns up immediately.

The emphasis game

Say je mange avec mes amis ce soir five times, stressing a different word each time. It's an acting exercise, not a language one — while your brain is busy deciding where the emphasis goes, the French comes out correctly without supervision. That's the whole point.
