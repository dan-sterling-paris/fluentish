Lesson 4 - 25 May 2026

Off Script - Fluency in the Wild

Buying time + BANGS rule + the Plus trap + Parisian reactions
What You Covered

First real free-form conversation in French - no script. The focus was on tools to stay in French when you get stuck rather than falling back to English: time-buying phrases, the BANGS adjective rule, a key pronunciation trap with "plus", and the reactions that make you sound properly Parisian.

The Buying Time Toolkit

Different from the Talk Back Toolkit. This one is for when you don't know a word - use these to stay in French rather than going silent or switching to English.

Comment on dit… ?
com-on on dee
How does one say…? Ask for any word in French.
C'est quoi en français ?
say kwah on fron-say
What's that in French?
C'est quoi en anglais ?
say kwah on on-glay
What's that in English? (last resort if you draw a complete blank)
Pour dire…
poor deer
To say… (when reaching for a word mid-sentence)
Donc…
donk
So… (filler - buys thinking time. Dan uses it without thinking.)
[the puff]
pfff
A legitimate French thinking noise. Use it freely.

"Comment on dit…?" keeps the conversation in French and sounds completely natural - as if you're searching for the right word, not giving up.

The BANGS Rule

Adjectives normally go after the noun in French - except for these five categories, which always go before.

B
Beauty
beau, joli
A
Age
jeune, vieux
N
Number
premier, deux
G
Goodness
bon, mauvais
S
Size
grand, petit
un grand vina great wine
un petit peua little bit
la première foisthe first time
un bon fromagea good cheese
un bel hommea handsome man ("beau" becomes "bel" before a vowel)

Cultural anecdote

The First World War in French is La Première Guerre mondiale - fine. But the Second World War is La Seconde Guerre mondiale, not deuxième. In French, "second" implies it is the last in a series (no third will follow), while "deuxième" implies more could come. Using "seconde" is a subtle statement of hope. Ask a French person why - they'll love it.

The Plus Trap - Important!

Spelled identically. The S changes everything.

Plus (say the S)
ploos
MORE.
"Je voudrais plus de fromage."
I'd like more cheese.
Plus (silent S)
plue
NO MORE.
"Je ne veux plus de fromage."
I don't want any more cheese.

Tip: think of it as saying a fuller, longer word when you mean more - that way you'll naturally keep the S.

"Encore !" for another round is actually more French than "plus" - unambiguous, and completely natural.

Il y a… - In the Wild

You covered this in Lesson 3. Here's where it really earns its keep.

Il y a une table en terrasse ?Is there a table on the terrace?
Il y a du paracétamol ?Do you have paracetamol? (at a pharmacy)
Il y a un bus pour le centre-ville ?Is there a bus to the city centre?
Il y a de la bière ?Is there beer? (de la = feminine)
Il y a du pain ?Is there bread? (du = masculine)

The warning

Don't use "Il y a…" when the thing is obviously right there in front of you. Walking into an empty restaurant and asking "Il y a une table ?" will get you a puzzled look.

Sounding Parisian

Three things that signal you've been around.

Chais pas shay pa

Parisians almost never say the full "Je ne sais pas" in casual conversation. Always with a slight shrug and throw-away delivery. Do this once and they'll know you've been around.

Ah bon ? a bon

Almost always a question - a touch of genuine surprise. A slight lift at the end. "Ah bon !" (statement) means agreement; "Ah bon ?" (question) means "Oh really?" Use the question version when something surprises you.

Bah non / Bah si ba non / ba see

"Bah non" = no way / oh come on. "Bah si" = oh but yes! (used to contradict a negative - the French equivalent of "oh yes it is"). Not a warm or friendly sound - it's a bit confrontational. Save it for when you're mildly outraged or pushing back.
